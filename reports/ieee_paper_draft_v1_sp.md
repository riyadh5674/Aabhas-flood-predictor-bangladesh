Leakage-Aware Probabilistic Flood Forecasting for Bangladesh: A Spatiotemporal Evaluation of Machine Learning and Deep Learning Models

Author Name(s) Omitted for Blind Review

Abstract—Flood-forecasting models can report optimistic performance when temporal dependence, station overlap, or forward-looking features introduce information leakage. This study presents a leakage-aware framework for probabilistic flood forecasting in Bangladesh that estimates whether a Flood Forecasting and Warning Centre (FFWC) station will reach or exceed its station-specific danger level within 24, 48, or 72 h. The modelling dataset contains 200,665 station-day records from 133 usable stations and integrates river water levels with meteorological, geospatial, upstream-discharge, soil-wetness, and neighbouring-station information. Predictors are restricted to information available at forecast issue time, and evaluation uses chronological validation, rolling-origin refits, station-disjoint spatial folds, and a frozen post-2022 test period. Classical gradient-boosting models are compared with recurrent, temporal-convolutional, fusion, graph-based, and Transformer models under a common scoring framework, with hydrological persistence as a mandatory benchmark. On the primary validation cell, the governed per-horizon model achieved a mean PR-AUC of 0.9574; on the frozen test cells it achieved 0.7708 versus 0.6676 for persistence. In the controlled ML-DL comparison, the best classical model reached 0.9546 compared with 0.9356 for the best deep model. The governed model also retained low calibration error on the test period. These results show that rigorous spatiotemporal validation and strong baselines are essential for credible flood-risk forecasting and that greater model complexity does not necessarily yield better generalization.

Keywords—Bangladesh, calibration, data leakage, deep learning, danger-level exceedance, FFWC, flood forecasting, machine learning, probabilistic forecasting, spatiotemporal validation.

I. INTRODUCTION

Flooding is one of the most recurrent and damaging natural hazards in Bangladesh, where the low-lying deltaic landscape and the interconnected Ganges–Brahmaputra–Meghna river system expose large populations to seasonal riverine flooding. Timely forecasting of rising river levels is therefore important for preparedness, evacuation planning, protection of livelihoods, and disaster-risk reduction. In Bangladesh, the Flood Forecasting and Warning Centre (FFWC) of the Bangladesh Water Development Board monitors river conditions through a network of hydrological stations and defines station-specific danger levels that provide an operational reference for flood conditions. For an early-warning application, however, estimating the future river stage alone is not necessarily the final decision problem. A more directly interpretable question is: What is the probability that a particular monitoring station will reach or exceed its danger level within the next 24, 48, or 72 hours?

Advances in machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) have created new opportunities for data-driven hydrological forecasting. Random forests, gradient-boosting algorithms, artificial neural networks, recurrent neural networks, long short-term memory (LSTM) networks, attention mechanisms, and hybrid architectures have been applied to river-stage, discharge, and flood forecasting [1]–[3]. Studies conducted in Bangladesh have similarly demonstrated the potential of ML and DL for predicting water levels and hydrological conditions using temporal, meteorological, and spatial information [4]–[8]. Deep sequence models are attractive because they can represent temporal dependencies directly, whereas tree-based ensemble models can effectively exploit nonlinear interactions among engineered hydrological, meteorological, temporal, and geographical predictors. Consequently, model selection should be based on robust out-of-sample evidence rather than an assumption that increased architectural complexity necessarily provides superior forecasting skill.

Reliable evaluation of flood-forecasting models is particularly challenging because hydrological observations are both temporally and spatially dependent. Randomly partitioning station-day observations can place closely related records from the same station or hydrological period in both training and test sets, potentially producing optimistic estimates of generalization. Feature engineering can introduce an additional source of leakage when rolling statistics, future observations, target-derived information, or improperly aligned meteorological variables cross the forecast issue-time boundary. Furthermore, a model that performs well at stations represented during training may not retain the same skill at previously unseen monitoring locations. These considerations make leakage control, chronological validation, spatial holdout evaluation, and strict enforcement of information availability at forecast issue time essential for credible assessment.

Another challenge concerns the interpretation of apparently high predictive performance. River water level is strongly persistent; therefore, the current hydrological state itself provides a powerful indication of near-future danger-level exceedance. An ML or DL model should consequently demonstrate added predictive value over a strong persistence-based reference rather than only outperforming other learning algorithms. In addition, danger-level exceedance constitutes an imbalanced prediction problem for many station-period combinations. Overall classification accuracy can therefore be misleading, while ROC-based measures alone may not adequately represent performance on the comparatively infrequent positive class. For an early-warning-oriented system, missed exceedances, precision–recall performance, probability calibration, and the reliability of risk thresholds are particularly important.

This study addresses these methodological issues through a leakage-aware framework for probabilistic multi-horizon flood forecasting in Bangladesh. The prediction unit is one FFWC station on one issue day. Using only information available up to that issue time, separate models estimate the probability that the station will reach or exceed its station-specific danger level during the subsequent 24-, 48-, and 72-h forecast windows. A positive label is assigned only when an observed water-level measurement reaches the defined danger level within the corresponding future window; incomplete future observations are not automatically interpreted as non-flood conditions. The modelling dataset covers 133 usable FFWC stations and integrates observed river water levels with meteorological, upstream, temporal, and geospatial information. The framework explicitly enforces backward-looking feature construction and evaluates performance across both temporal and spatial generalization settings.

Rather than proposing another forecasting architecture, this work investigates a more fundamental question: under a common, leakage-controlled experimental framework, do increasingly complex ML and DL models provide reliable improvements for probabilistic danger-level exceedance forecasting across forecast horizons and locations? Classical gradient-boosted models are compared with sequence-, attention-, and graph-based DL approaches using consistent data partitions and evaluation procedures. A water-level persistence model is retained as a mandatory reference benchmark. Performance is assessed primarily through precision–recall behaviour, recall and false-negative characteristics, probability calibration, and robustness across temporal periods and spatial holdouts, complemented by model explanations to determine whether learned relationships are hydrologically plausible.

The principal contributions of this work are as follows:

1. Probabilistic multi-horizon formulation: The study predicts the probability of station-specific FFWC danger-level exceedance within 24-, 48-, and 72-h windows, rather than treating flood prediction as same-day classification or relying exclusively on continuous water-level regression.

2. Leakage-aware spatiotemporal validation: All features obey the forecast issue-time boundary, while chronological, rolling-origin, station-disjoint, unseen-station, and seasonal evaluations are used to examine future-time and spatial generalization.

3. Controlled ML-DL comparison against a strong hydrological baseline: Classical ensemble methods and multiple sequence-, graph-, and attention-based DL architectures are evaluated through common partitions and scoring procedures, with water-level persistence retained as the mandatory reference.

4. Risk-oriented and interpretable evaluation: The study emphasizes PR-AUC, recall, missed exceedances, calibration, and horizon-specific robustness, and uses feature attribution and graded risk interpretation to assess whether predictions are both statistically credible and hydrologically plausible.

The resulting framework is intended as a research-oriented foundation for location-aware flood-risk decision support rather than a replacement for official FFWC forecasts or warnings. By emphasizing leakage prevention, realistic spatiotemporal validation, strong baselines, calibrated probabilities, and transparent comparison of model families, the study seeks to provide a more defensible assessment of what data-driven methods can—and cannot—contribute to short-term flood-risk forecasting in Bangladesh.

II. RELATED WORK AND RESEARCH GAP

Data-driven flood forecasting has received increasing attention as machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) methods provide flexible alternatives to conventional hydrological models for learning nonlinear relationships among rainfall, river stage, discharge, meteorological variables, and catchment characteristics. Recent reviews show extensive use of artificial neural networks, support vector machines, random forests (RF), gradient-boosting methods, long short-term memory (LSTM) networks, and hybrid architectures for flood and water-level forecasting [1], [2]. LSTM-based approaches are particularly prominent because of their ability to represent temporal dependencies, while ensemble and hybrid approaches have also demonstrated competitive performance across different forecasting horizons [1]. Nevertheless, comparative studies indicate that no single model family is consistently superior across catchments, data conditions, and lead times, emphasizing the importance of evaluation design and appropriate baselines rather than model complexity alone [1]–[3].

Several recent studies have investigated ML- and DL-based hydrological forecasting in Bangladesh. An attention-based study combining spatial and temporal information used ANN, LSTM, spatial-attention LSTM, temporal-attention LSTM, and spatiotemporal-attention LSTM models to forecast river water levels at selected stations in Bangladesh, demonstrating improved performance when spatial and temporal attention were combined [4]. More recent work on the Old Brahmaputra River compared classical ML and DL approaches for water-level prediction and reported strong performance from RF, XGBoost, LightGBM, and LSTM depending on the predictor scenario and transfer station [5]. Comparative studies of extreme river levels have similarly shown that ensemble ML methods, particularly RF, can perform strongly across Bangladesh river stations [6]. Other multi-horizon studies have compared ML and recurrent DL models for river-stage or discharge forecasting and examined their spatial transferability, demonstrating that relative model performance can change with forecast horizon and prediction location [7], [8].

These studies establish the potential of data-driven methods for hydrological forecasting in Bangladesh; however, most formulate the problem as continuous water-level, stage, or discharge regression. Such forecasts are valuable hydrologically but differ from the operational question addressed in this work: the probability that a monitored station will reach or exceed its defined danger level within a future 24-, 48-, or 72-h window. A probabilistic exceedance formulation directly supports graded risk interpretation while avoiding the assumption that small improvements in continuous-value prediction necessarily translate into better flood-warning decisions.

More broadly, methodological reviews have identified persistent limitations in data-driven flood forecasting, including inconsistent validation strategies, limited assessment of spatial transferability, insufficient uncertainty characterization, heterogeneous performance metrics, and difficulties in comparing models trained and evaluated under different experimental conditions [1], [2]. Random train–test splitting is particularly problematic for spatiotemporal hydrological data because temporal dependence or repeated observations from the same monitoring locations can allow closely related information to occur in both training and evaluation sets. Recent hydrological forecasting research has consequently emphasized chronological or rolling-origin evaluation and independent holdout periods to obtain more realistic estimates of generalization [9]. Reproducible comparisons are further complicated when different model families use different data partitions, feature sets, tuning budgets, or metric implementations.

Another limitation concerns the choice of reference model. River stage is strongly autocorrelated, making persistence a difficult but operationally meaningful benchmark. A sophisticated ML or DL model may achieve apparently high predictive performance while adding little value over the most recently observed hydrological state. Therefore, performance should be evaluated not only among competing learning algorithms but also relative to a strong persistence baseline. Furthermore, because flood exceedances are relatively infrequent, conventional accuracy and even ROC-based measures can obscure performance on the event class. Precision–recall measures, false-negative analysis, probability calibration, and operationally meaningful decision thresholds are consequently important when the intended output is an early-warning probability rather than merely a continuous hydrological estimate.

A. Research Gap

Despite substantial progress, there remains limited evidence from Bangladesh from a single leakage-controlled experimental framework that simultaneously evaluates classical ML and modern DL models for probabilistic, multi-horizon danger-level exceedance forecasting. Existing studies have generally concentrated on water-level or discharge regression, individual river systems or stations, and model-specific performance comparisons. Comparatively less attention has been given to whether reported performance remains robust under both future-time evaluation and transfer to unseen monitoring stations, whether more complex DL architectures provide measurable advantages over carefully engineered tabular ML models under identical experimental conditions, and whether predicted probabilities remain sufficiently calibrated for risk-oriented interpretation.

This study addresses these gaps by formulating flood forecasting as a station-day probabilistic prediction problem: using only information available at issue time, the model estimates whether an FFWC monitoring station will reach its station-specific danger level within the subsequent 24, 48, or 72 h. The experimental framework explicitly prevents forward-looking feature leakage, separates temporal and spatial evaluation, and compares classical ML, sequence-based DL, graph-based and attention-based models through a common scoring pathway and consistent tuning conditions. A strong water-level persistence predictor is retained as the mandatory reference benchmark rather than relying solely on inter-model comparisons. Evaluation emphasizes PR-AUC, recall and missed floods, probability calibration, temporal and spatial robustness, and explainability in addition to conventional discrimination measures. In this way, the work shifts the emphasis from obtaining the highest score on a single split toward determining whether learned flood-risk signals remain credible, transferable, calibrated, and hydrologically interpretable under realistic spatiotemporal evaluation conditions.

III. DATASET AND FORECAST FORMULATION

A. Data Sources and Study Coverage

The study integrates hydrological, meteorological, upstream-discharge, soil-moisture, and geospatial information to construct a station-day forecasting dataset for Bangladesh. The primary hydrological data were obtained from the Flood Forecasting and Warning Centre (FFWC) of the Bangladesh Water Development Board. The FFWC station master provided station identity, coordinates, and station-specific danger levels, while approximately 1.05 million 3-hourly water-level observations supplied the primary dynamic predictor and the basis for target construction. Of the 153 stations in the station master, 141 contained observed water-level records and 133 had a usable numeric danger level and at least one valid forecast label; these 133 stations constitute the effective modelling network. The usable record extends from January 2017 to October 2026, with 2018 absent from the FFWC archive and therefore excluded rather than interpolated.

Meteorological variables were obtained from NASA POWER/MERRA-2 and include daily precipitation and temperature information aligned to the monitoring stations. Static geographical characteristics were derived from SRTM elevation data, OpenStreetMap river networks, and geoBoundaries administrative layers. These sources provided elevation, terrain, distance-to-river, river-density, and administrative-context variables. The extended feature set additionally incorporated upstream/transboundary discharge from Open-Meteo GloFAS and station-level soil-wetness variables from NASA POWER. ERA5-Land variables were experimentally evaluated but were not retained because they did not improve validation performance.

The 3-hourly FFWC observations were aggregated to daily station records using maximum, mean, minimum, and last-observed water level together with daily observation counts. A complete station-by-calendar-day matrix was first generated so that missing dates remained explicit. Invalid and sentinel observations were removed during quality control, while the complete 2018 data gap was preserved as missing. The resulting model-ready dataset contains 200,665 station-day records from 133 stations.

B. Forecast Formulation and Label Construction

The forecasting task was formulated as a probabilistic danger-level exceedance problem rather than continuous water-level regression or contemporaneous flood classification. Each sample corresponds to one FFWC station (s) and one issue day (t). Using information available up to and including the end of day (t), the objective is to estimate the probability that the station reaches or exceeds its station-specific FFWC danger level within a future horizon (h), where h ∈ {24, 48, 72} h.

For station (s), issue day (t), and forecast horizon (h), the target was defined as

yₛ,ₜ⁽ʰ⁾ = { 1, if any observed water level in (t, t+h] ≥ Dₛ;  0, if the future window is sufficiently observed and no reading reaches Dₛ;  NaN, otherwise. }

where (D_s) denotes the station-specific FFWC danger level. A negative label was assigned only when every day in the forecast window contained at least three observations and the danger level was known. Consequently, missing future observations were never interpreted as evidence of no flooding. Positive labels were retained whenever an observed exceedance occurred, even if other observations in the future window were missing. The logical consistency condition

y⁽²⁴⁾ = 1  ⇒  y⁽⁴⁸⁾ = 1  ⇒  y⁽⁷²⁾ = 1

was additionally checked during dataset validation.

After application of the label contract, 200,559, 197,748, and 195,174 records were labelled at the 24-, 48-, and 72-h horizons, respectively, containing 9,982, 11,241, and 12,340 positive samples. Corresponding overall event rates were 4.98%, 5.68%, and 6.32%. However, prevalence varied substantially over time, season, and station. For example, the 72-h positive rate declined from 16.81% in 2017 to 1.19% in 2023, while the monsoon-period rate was approximately 5.4 times that of the remainder of the year. These variations motivated cell-specific and season-specific evaluation rather than reliance on a single pooled performance estimate.

IV. METHODOLOGY

A. Feature Engineering and Leakage Control

All predictors were constrained by a strict forecast issue-time information boundary: a feature for issue day (t) could depend only on information available at or before (t). No observation from the future target window ((t,t+h]) was permitted to contribute to the predictor matrix.

The initial full feature representation contained 62 predictors grouped into meteorological variables, rainfall history, static geography, station-level hydrology, and neighbouring-station/network information. Hydrological features included current and lagged water-level characteristics, backward-looking trends and rolling statistics, and danger-relative variables such as the current percentage of danger level and distance from the station-specific danger threshold. Rainfall-history variables summarized antecedent precipitation, while geographical predictors described terrain and proximity to river networks. Network features captured hydrological conditions at neighbouring monitoring locations.

The final extended feature representation, denoted FULL_V2, contains 110 predictors. In addition to the original feature groups, it incorporates 19 soil-wetness variables, 18 GloFAS-derived upstream-discharge variables, six additional water-level memory/API variables, and five interaction features. All added variables underwent the same leakage audit as the original feature set; backward temporal shifts were enforced, and climatological quantities were calculated exclusively from pre-validation data.

A formal leakage audit was conducted before model comparison. Station identifiers, dates, source descriptors, all target labels, and future-observation-count variables were excluded from the predictor space. Every lag, rolling statistic, and aggregate was verified to use only observations available at or before the issue day. Preprocessing operations—including median imputation, frequency encoding, and standardization—were fitted exclusively on the corresponding training partition. Target encoding was not used. For sequence models, temporal windows were constrained to station identity and the corresponding split/fold so that sequences could not cross temporal, spatial, holdout, or seasonal boundaries.

B. Temporal and Spatial Validation

Random train–test partitioning was explicitly avoided because it could allow temporally adjacent observations or records from the same stations to appear on both sides of the evaluation boundary. The primary chronological partition comprised training data through 2021, validation data from 2022, and a frozen test period from 2023 onward. The principal model-selection cell, val_2022, contains 17,538 non-holdout validation records, while the frozen test set contains 101,174 records. The temporal boundaries fall within naturally occurring dry-season gaps of 88–131 days with no model-ready observations; an additional seven-day embargo was retained as a safeguard against forecast-label windows crossing a split boundary. This design follows recommendations for blocked validation when temporal or spatial dependence is present [10].

Spatial generalization was evaluated using five station-disjoint folds balanced by label count and station-network age. Fold 0, comprising 26 stations and 19,763 test-period records, was reserved as the frozen unseen-station holdout. Folds 1–4 were used during development to measure transfer to stations excluded from the corresponding model fit. The post-2022 test period was further separated into trained stations, new-network stations, and unseen holdout stations, preventing stations that entered the FFWC record only after the training period from being incorrectly classified as previously observed locations.

Five complementary evaluation protocols were defined. P1 used the pre-2022 training data and val_2022 for primary model selection. P2 implemented rolling-origin temporal evaluation by refitting the model using years preceding each evaluation year. P3 implemented station-disjoint spatial cross-validation by refitting on four folds and evaluating on the remaining fold. P4 represented the frozen post-2022 unseen-station evaluation, while P5 stratified performance by monsoon (June–September) and non-monsoon periods. Importantly, P2 and P3 required a separate model refit for every evaluation cell rather than scoring multiple cells using a single globally fitted model.

C. Baselines and Machine-Learning Models

A hierarchy of reference models was established before evaluating more complex algorithms. This included climatology, a static-geography-only reference, logistic-regression variants, a shallow decision tree, and a hydrological persistence model. The persistence benchmark, denoted D7, uses the station's current water level relative to its danger level and was designated the mandatory performance bar because short-term river stage is highly autocorrelated. Thus, improvements were interpreted relative to a strong hydrological reference rather than merely relative to other learning algorithms.

Five classical tree-based model configurations were subsequently evaluated under a fixed tuning budget: LightGBM, XGBoost, an XGBoost model using the compact selected feature set, Random Forest, and Gradient Boosting. Separate models were trained for the 24-, 48-, and 72-h targets because the best-performing algorithm and feature relationships differed by forecast horizon. The final governed configuration uses LightGBM for the 24- and 48-h horizons and XGBoost for 72 h on the 110-variable FULL_V2 representation.

D. Deep-Learning Models and Fair Model Comparison

The DL experiments were designed to test whether architectures capable of explicitly representing temporal or spatial dependencies could improve upon the engineered tabular models. Recurrent sequence models included LSTM and GRU, while temporal convolutional networks (TCNs) provided a convolution-based sequence alternative. Sequence lengths of 3, 7, 14, and 30 days were investigated using explicit missingness masks.

A fusion architecture concatenated the learned sequence representation with the engineered tabular feature vector. Spatial relationships among stations were additionally investigated using gated graph neural networks, including graph-convolutional and graph-attention variants, while a gated Transformer was evaluated for attention-based sequence representation.

To avoid giving either model family an experimental advantage, the final ML–DL comparison used identical temporal and spatial partitions, common random seeds, the same metric implementation, and comparable tuning conditions. Twenty-four candidate configurations—six baselines, five classical models, and thirteen sequence/graph/DL models—were re-scored through a single evaluation pathway over the development cells. Models required by the rolling-origin and spatial protocols were refitted independently for each task. This common evaluation framework was used to determine whether increased architectural complexity yielded reproducible gains rather than comparing scores obtained from heterogeneous experimental settings.

E. Probability Calibration and Risk Interpretation

Because the intended output is a flood-risk probability rather than only a binary classification, probability calibration was evaluated explicitly. Calibration quality was assessed using the Brier score, reliability diagrams, and expected calibration error (ECE) computed using ten equal-width probability bins. An ECE of 0.02 was adopted as the prespecified calibration tolerance. Where necessary, five-fold out-of-fold isotonic regression and Platt scaling were evaluated as post-hoc calibration procedures, with calibration models estimated without access to the corresponding evaluation observations. These are established post-hoc probability-calibration approaches [12].

For decision-oriented interpretation, predicted probabilities were also mapped to four descriptive risk categories—low, moderate, high, and very high. Operating thresholds were derived on the validation data using a cost-sensitive objective in which a missed danger-level exceedance was provisionally assigned ten times the cost of a false alarm:

J(τ) = 10 FN(τ) + FP(τ)

where (\tau) denotes the candidate probability threshold. Sensitivity analyses considered 5:1 and 20:1 cost ratios. Thresholds were estimated separately by horizon and seasonal context and frozen before application to the test period. These costs represent research assumptions rather than stakeholder-validated operational preferences; consequently, the resulting risk bands are treated as decision-support communication categories rather than official FFWC warning levels.

F. Evaluation Metrics

Because danger-level exceedance is relatively infrequent and its prevalence changes substantially over time, precision–recall area under the curve (PR-AUC) was selected as the principal discrimination metric, since precision–recall analysis is particularly informative for imbalanced binary outcomes [11]. PR-AUC was computed separately for each forecast horizon, temporal evaluation cell, spatial fold, and seasonal subset rather than only on pooled observations. ROC-AUC was retained as a complementary discrimination measure.

Operationally relevant classification measures included recall, precision, false negatives (missed exceedances), and false alarms at validation-derived operating thresholds. Calibration was assessed using the Brier score and ECE. Station-level and cell-specific reporting was retained because positive events are spatially concentrated and because event prevalence varies markedly between years and seasons. Where applicable, model comparisons were interpreted relative to the D7 persistence benchmark rather than from absolute scores alone.

For the final warning-oriented assessment, event-level measures were additionally considered, including the proportion of distinct exceedance events detected, number of missed events, warning lead time, and false alarms per event. These measures complement station-day discrimination metrics by assessing whether statistically accurate probability forecasts translate into practically useful advance warning. Model interpretability was examined using TreeSHAP for the leading tree-based models, with feature attribution summarized by hydrological, meteorological, geographical, neighbouring-station, upstream-discharge, and soil-moisture feature groups.
